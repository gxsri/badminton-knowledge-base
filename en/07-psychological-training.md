# Psychological Training — A Diagnostic Guide

> **For**: Athletes and coaches
> **Core philosophy**: Psychology is not about "being tough" — it's about "which weakness, and how to fix it." Just like physical training: diagnose first, then train.

## Chapter 1: Psychological Training Is Not "Be Strong"

### 1.1 The Four Trainable Dimensions of Psychology

Mental ability is not innate. Like strength, it breaks down into different dimensions, each of which can be trained individually.

| Dimension | Corresponding Problem | Analogy |
|:----------|:---------------------|:--------|
| **Attention Control** | Getting distracted during matches, zone-outs, overthinking | Like a camera's autofocus system |
| **Pressure Adaptation** | Choking in key moments, technique breaking down | Like progressive overload for muscles |
| **Self-Regulation** | Emotional meltdowns after mistakes, chain errors | Like a car's stability control system |
| **Decision Confidence** | Hesitation, not taking shots you should take | Like a navigation system's route selection |

### 1.2 Diagnostic Method: Find Your Weak Spot First

The four tests below correspond to the four dimensions. Record your results for each.

## Chapter 2: Attention Control — Like a Camera's Autofocus System

### 2.1 What Is Your Attention Type?

Attention is not about "how long you can focus." First figure out which type of problem you have.

**Self-Check (no coach needed, you can do it yourself):**

```
Scenario 1: You're practicing alone. 10 minutes in —
  A. Still fully focused
  B. Starting to think about other things
  C. Zoned out long ago, movements have become mechanical
→ If A: Your attention endurance is fine — the problem is your attention type
→ If B or C: Your attention endurance needs training

Scenario 2: Someone nearby is talking/walking around —
  A. Completely unaffected
  B. Gets distracted but can pull back
  C. Fully disrupted
→ If C: Your selective attention needs training

Scenario 3: Doing footwork while watching a coach's hand signals —
  A. Can do both simultaneously
  B. Footwork is correct but missed the signal, or saw the signal but footwork fell apart
  C. Can't do either well
→ If B or C: Your divided attention needs training
```

### 2.2 Attention Training Starting Volume Formula

```
Training duration = Time you can currently hold stable focus × 70%

How to find out "how long I can currently hold stable focus"?
Test method: Watch the second hand on a clock, try not to let your attention drift
From the moment you realize "I'm starting to focus" to when you notice "I zoned out"
Record this time

Example: You can focus for 2 minutes → Starting training = 2 × 70% = 1 minute 24 seconds
Example: You can focus for 8 minutes → Starting training = 8 × 70% = 5 minutes 36 seconds
```

**Progression Rules:**
```
Able to complete 3 consecutive training sessions at starting duration without zoning out → Add 30 seconds
Zone out for 2 consecutive training sessions → Go back to the last stable duration
```

### 2.3 Different Training Methods for Different Attention Types

| Your Self-Check Result | Training Method | Principle |
|:-----------------------|:----------------|:----------|
| Poor endurance | Single-point stare: fix your gaze on a spot on the wall, restart the clock when you zone out | Extend "sustainable focus" duration |
| Poor selectivity | Train under distraction: turn on a TV/play music first, then do your technical training | Teach your brain to filter information |
| Poor divided attention | Dual-task: do footwork while counting (or responding to random coach questions) | Build neural pathways for multitasking |

## Chapter 3: Pressure Adaptation — Not Toughing It Out, But Graded Exposure

### 3.1 Where Is Your Pressure Threshold

**Pressure Heart Rate Test (no heart rate monitor needed, go by feel):**

```
Calm state: Your natural breathing rhythm, no effort
L1 Low Pressure: Someone watches you play
L2 Moderate Pressure: Timing/scoring requirements
L3 High Pressure: Critical points (e.g., 19-19)
L4 Extreme Pressure: Critical points + already fatigued

Your pressure level = the level at which your technique starts to break down

Self-check method:
Recall your most recent match/training session —
At what point did your technique start falling apart?
- Someone was watching? → L2
- Scoring started? → L3
- Critical points? → L4
```

### 3.2 Pressure Adaptation Training Design Formula

```
You cannot jump directly to high pressure to train.

Training pressure level = Your pressure threshold - 1

Example: You break down at L3 (competitive scoring)
→ You should now train at L2 (someone watches, but no scoring)
→ Once L2 is stable (technique holds for 3 consecutive sessions) → Move up to L3
→ Once L3 is stable → Move up to L4
```

**How to Control Pressure Levels (Coach's Guide):**

| Pressure Level | What the Coach Does |
|:---------------|:--------------------|
| L1 No Pressure | Alone, closed environment, no timing/evaluation |
| L2 Low Pressure | Add one observer, no comments, just watching |
| L3 Moderate Pressure | Set a target (e.g., 7 out of 10 shuttles must reach the backcourt), consequences if not met (push-ups) |
| L4 High Pressure | Simulated match scoring, critical point scenarios |
| L5 Extreme | L4 + exhausted state (do physical training to fatigue first, then do technical training) |

### 3.3 Diagnosing Technical Breakdown Under Pressure

Technique breaking down is not "weak mentality." Don't ask why — first rule things out:

```
You're having issues under pressure —

Step 1: Is the technique unstable by itself, or does it only break down under pressure?
   → Technique is unstable by itself → Go back to no-pressure state and train technique
   → Only breaks down under pressure → Go to Step 2

Step 2: Which dimension is breaking down?
   → Contact point is late → Attention is consumed by pressure → Train attention distribution
   → Movement speeds up (rhythm is off) → Pressure is causing urgency → Build a "between-point ritual"
   → Body gets stiff → Pressure is causing muscle tension → Train breathing control
```

## Chapter 4: Self-Regulation — How to Break the Chain Reaction After a Mistake

### 4.1 The Cognitive Chain After a Mistake

```
Mistake → Thought appears → Emotion appears → Physical response → Next movement breaks down

Most people get stuck at the "thought appears" step.

What the thoughts are:
"Why did I make that mistake again"
"Not my day today"
"That was such an easy shot"
→ These are all unproductive thoughts
```

**The difference between productive and unproductive thoughts:**

| Unproductive Thought | → | Productive Thought |
|:--------------------|:-:|:-------------------|
| "Why did I make that mistake again" | | "I need to watch my contact point on this shot next time" |
| "Not my day today" | | "I didn't warm up enough today, I'll add 5 more minutes of activation before the match" |
| "That was such an easy shot" | | "The easier the shot, the more I need to complete the full motion" |
| "I lost again" | | "What did I learn from this match" |

### 4.2 Build Your Own "Interrupt Button"

You can't prevent mistakes from happening, but you can insert an interruption between the mistake and the next action.

**Designing Your Interrupt Button:**

```
Step 1: Choose a physical action (not a mental one)
   Good choices: Touch the string, adjust your wristband, take a deep breath
   Bad choices: Think "I need to calm down" (thinking doesn't interrupt emotions)

Step 2: Lock it in — do the same action every time after a mistake
   This will build in your brain:
   Mistake → Touch string → Next shot

Step 3: Use it in training, don't wait for matches
   Do this action after every mistake in practice → It will trigger automatically in matches
```

### 4.3 Response Formulas for Different Emotional States

| Your State | How to Tell | Intervention |
|:-----------|:------------|:-------------|
| **Too excited (heart rate too high)** | Racing heart, slight hand tremors, moving faster than normal | Deep breathing (4-7-8 breathing technique to lower heart rate) |
| **Too sluggish (heart rate too low)** | Feeling flat, scattered attention, moving slower than normal | Jumping jacks / high knees for 30 seconds (raise heart rate) |
| **Too tense (muscles stiff)** | Grip too tight, shoulders hunched, shallow breathing | Drop shoulders, wrist circles, lengthen exhale |
| **Too deflated (low mood)** | Head down after mistakes, sighing, not talking | Interrupt button (touch string) + 3 deep breaths |

## Chapter 5: Decision Confidence — The Cost of Hesitation

### 5.1 What Kind of Hesitation Do You Have

| Hesitation Type | Signs | Cause | Training Method |
|:----------------|:------|:------|:----------------|
| **Information overload** | Thinking too much, analyzing every possibility until it's too late | Too many options | Limit your options (only prepare 2 choices during a match) |
| **Fear of being wrong** | You know what to hit, but you're afraid you'll mess it up so you don't hit it | Risk aversion | Accept 70% success rate and execute — don't demand 100% |
| **Lack of experience** | Don't know what to choose | Haven't seen enough scenarios | Watch more match videos to build a pattern library |

### 5.2 The 3-2-1 Decision Training Method

```
3 seconds to observe → 2 seconds to decide → 1 second to execute

Training method:
1. Coach/partner stands at random positions on the other side of the court
2. You must observe their position within 3 seconds
3. Decide which direction to hit within 2 seconds
4. Execute the shot within 1 second

First 10 reps: Hitting wrong is allowed — focus on "decision speed" not "decision accuracy"
After 10 reps: Start incorporating "decision accuracy" requirements

Progression benchmarks:
Complete the full 3-2-1 sequence within 7 seconds → Passing
Complete it within 5 seconds → Good
Complete it within 3 seconds → Match level
```

## Chapter 6: Pre-Match Psychological Preparation — Not "Just Relax"

### 6.1 Arousal Level Adjustment (Different People Need Different Arousal Levels)

Not everyone needs to "relax." Some people need to "get fired up."

| Your Pre-Match State | Your Arousal Level | Need |
|:--------------------|:-------------------|:-----|
| Too tense (stiff muscles, cold hands) | Too high | Lower arousal |
| Normal (slight excitement, focused) | Just right | Maintain |
| Too sluggish (no energy, don't want to talk, sleepy) | Too low | Raise arousal |

**Arousal Level Regulation Formula:**

```
Lowering arousal:
- Deep breathing (4-7-8 breathing technique, 4 rounds)
- Slow walking for 2 minutes
- Listen to calm music

Raising arousal:
- Jumping jacks / high knees for 30 seconds
- Shout out loud / slap your thighs
- Listen to fast-paced music / watch highlight match clips
```

### 6.2 Pre-Match Routine (Not Fixed — It's Your Personal Routine)

A good pre-match routine must include three elements:

| Phase | Time | What to Do | Purpose |
|:------|:----:|:-----------|:--------|
| **Physical preparation** | 30 min before match | Warm-up + activation | Get the body ready |
| **Attention preparation** | 10 min before match | A fixed rhythm: deep breath → key word → shadow swing | Lock the brain into match mode |
| **Execution preparation** | Before stepping on court | A fixed action (touch strings, deep breath, fixed gaze) | Trigger the "start" signal |

## Chapter 7: Post-Match Psychological Processing

### 7.1 Three Things to Do Immediately After a Match (Not "Rest")

| Order | What to Do | Why |
|:------|:-----------|:----|
| 1 | Write down 3 things "I did well" | The brain naturally reinforces negative information — you need to actively input positive information |
| 2 | Write down 3 "areas to improve" | Not to criticize yourself, but to give direction for the next training session |
| 3 | Give this match a "learning score" (1-10) | Score based on how much you learned, not on win/loss |

### 7.2 Distinguish Between "Technical Problems" and "Psychological Problems"

After a match, don't vaguely say "my mentality is bad." Ask yourself:

```
The reason I lost / played poorly today is:

1. Can I execute this technique in training?
   ● Yes → It's a psychological problem (inability to execute under pressure)
   ● No → It's a technical problem (the technique itself is unstable)

2. If it's a psychological problem:
   ● Was it losing focus?
   ● Was it getting nervous?
   ● Was it failing to reset after a mistake?
   → Match it to the dimension above, and train that dimension specifically next session
```

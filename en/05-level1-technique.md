# Level 1: Forehand Clear — Principles, Self-Diagnosis, Self-Practice

> **For**: Players whose clear is short, inaccurate, or leaves their arm sore
> **Goal**: After reading this, you can diagnose your own problem, correct it yourself, and design your own practice
> **Core idea**: A forehand clear is 80% power sequence and 20% muscle strength. You don't need to be "stronger" — you need the right order of power generation.

## Chapter 1: The Physics of the Clear — Three Conditions, All Required

### 1.1 A good clear needs three things simultaneously

```
A clear goes far = Racket head speed × Effective contact point × Complete kinetic chain

If any condition is not met → the shuttle won't go far.
```

**Condition ①: Enough racket head speed**

```
The speed you give the shuttle = the speed of the racket head at the moment of contact.

Where does racket head speed come from?
Not from "swinging hard" — from "transmitting speed from your feet to the racket head
through the entire kinetic chain."

This is like cracking a whip: the speed at the tip comes from the whole whip body
being swung. Your arm is the tip. Your torso and legs are the hand swinging the whip.
Arm only = using just the tip.
Full body = using the whole whip — the tip speed is 3-5 times faster.
```

**Condition ②: Effective contact point**

```
It's not "just touch the shuttle" — it's "touch the shuttle where your body
can generate maximum power."

Three requirements for an effective contact point:
1. Height: in front of and above the forehead (not above the head, not at ear level)
2. Forward-back: in front of the body (not directly above, not behind)
3. Left-right: on the racket-hand side (not in the middle of the body)

Self-check:
  Stand in your hitting stance. Place the shuttle where you think your
  best contact point is.
  Can you swing at maximum power from this position without hitting your body?
  Yes → your contact point is effective
  No → adjust your contact point
```

**Condition ③: Complete kinetic chain**

```
Power flows from ankle → knee → hip → waist → shoulder → elbow → wrist → racket face.
Every link participates, in sequence.

If any link "breaks" (doesn't participate or participates in wrong order):
→ the power reaching the racket face is reduced by that link's contribution
→ the arm has to "compensate" for the broken link
→ the arm compensates → the arm gets sore
```

## Chapter 2: Which Type of "Bad Clear" Are You?

### 2.1 Three types of problems (not five, not ten — just three)

If your clear is bad, the cause is one of only three possibilities.

```
- These three types cover 95% of all forehand clear problems
- Type A: Slow shuttle (the shuttle floats, no power)
- Type B: Low trajectory (flat, net-clipping, or dropping)
- Type C: Wrong direction (left/right unpredictable)
- Most people are "A+B" mixed — slow AND flat.
- A few are "C" — speed is OK but direction is uncontrolled.
```

**Self-test: Hit 5 clears to a partner. Ask them three questions:**

```
① "Did the shuttle still have power when it reached you?"
   No power → Type A (speed problem)
   Still had power but too high or too flat → Type B (trajectory problem)

② "Did the shuttle come toward you or to the side?"
   Off to the side → Type C (direction problem)

③ "How high was the shuttle when it reached you?"
   Above head → B1 (too high, good arc but overshooting)
   Waist height → B2 (too flat, not enough arc)
   Knee height → B3 (too low, almost landing)
```

**Find your type → jump to the corresponding chapter.** No need to read front to back.

## Chapter 3: Type A — Slow Shuttle (Kinetic Chain Problem)

### 3.1 Slow shuttle has only one cause: wrong power sequence

```
Hitting the shuttle is not "one hand swinging."
The correct feeling is "the whole body snapping."

If you feel:
- "I'm swinging hard but the shuttle has no power"
- "My arm is exhausted after just 10 clears"
- "I'm turning my hips and pushing my legs, but still can't hit far"

Then your power sequence is wrong.
```

### 3.2 The correct power sequence — from ground to racket face

One sentence to remember:

```
Feet generate power first → power travels upward → hip → waist → shoulder → arm moves last
```

**The five segments in simple terms:**

```
① Foot push (triple extension)
   Push the ball of the back foot into the ground, completing three actions
   simultaneously:
   Heel lifts (ankle extension), leg straightens (knee extension), glutes tighten (hip extension)
   This is called Triple Extension

② Hip rotation
   Push-off power travels forward through the hip.
   The hip turns from sideways to facing the net.
   It's NOT "twisting the waist" — it's "sending the back hip forward"

③ Waist/core rotation
   Hip rotation drives the upper body to follow.
   Chest turns from sideways to facing the net.
   Core (abs + lower back) stays tight throughout — if loose, power "leaks out"

④ Shoulder girdle
   Backswing: squeeze shoulder blades together (scapular retraction)
   → Hitting: push the arm forward (scapular protraction)
   → Shoulder internal rotation (like turning a screwdriver)

⑤ Arm + wrist (last 0.1 second)
   Before the first four segments complete, the arm stays relaxed (like a whip)
   0.1 second before contact: forearm pronation + wrist flexion
   → Grip tightens at the moment of contact
```

### 3.3 Self-check the five segments — check from bottom up, the first "No" is your root cause

```
Hit 5 clears. After each one, check:

☐ Did your back heel lift off the ground?
   No → you didn't use your ankle. Triple extension is missing the first link.
   Fix: imagine "reaching for a high light switch with your toes"

☐ Did your back leg go from bent to straight?
   No → your knee didn't participate.
   Fix: your leg should be bent before hitting and straight during the hit

☐ Did your glutes tighten?
   No → glutes didn't participate. Power breaks at the hip.
   Fix: think "squeeze your butt" before hitting

☐ Did your hip turn from sideways to facing the net?
   No → your hip didn't move; you were only rotating your upper body.
   Fix: think "push your right pocket forward" when hitting

☐ Is your chest facing the net after hitting?
   No → your waist didn't participate in rotation.
   Fix: think "pull your left shoulder back, push right shoulder forward"

☐ Did your upper back feel tight during backswing?
   No → you're backswinging with just the arm, not using the scapulae.
   Fix: imagine "squeezing a pen between your shoulder blades"

☐ Was your arm relaxed before hitting?
   No → your arm activated too early. You skipped the lower body.
   Fix: keep your arm like a rope until 0.1 second before contact
```

**Correction order is ALWAYS bottom to top:**
```
Find the first "No" (start checking from the feet)
→ Fix ONLY this one segment
→ Hit 5 clears and check the result
→ If it worked, check the next segment
→ If it didn't work, try a different fix for the same segment
```

### 3.4 Type A self-practice

```
Practice 1: Segmented shadow swing (train the power sequence)

Steps:
1. Stand in ready position, no racket
2. Do ONLY "foot push + hip rotation" — keep your arms still
3. Do 5 reps. Feel what it means for "lower body to move first"
4. Add "waist + shoulder" — arms still still
5. Do 5 reps. Feel power transmitting from lower body to torso
6. Finally add the arm — do complete shadow swings
7. Do 10 reps. After each one, check if your heel lifted

Check standard:
☐ After 10 shadow swings, arm not sore → power sequence is correct
☐ After 10 shadow swings, arm is sore → you're still using too much arm
```

```
Practice 2: Half-court short clears (train the timing)

Steps:
1. Stand near the service line
2. Only hit half-court clears (don't chase distance)
3. Use only 30% power
4. Focus on feeling: "foot first → then hip → then waist → then shoulder → then hand"
5. Once the sequence is correct, gradually move back and increase power

Why it works:
At half-court distance, you don't need full power to get the shuttle there.
This gives you "spare attention" to focus on the power sequence.
If you start at the baseline going full power, all your attention goes to
"getting the shuttle over" — you have no spare capacity for the sequence.
```

```
Practice 3: Racket head acceleration (feel the speed)

Steps:
1. Do shadow swings
2. Feel where the racket head is fastest
3. With correct power sequence, the fastest point should be at contact
4. If the racket head is already slowing down before contact → you're accelerating too early
5. If the racket head hasn't reached peak speed at contact → you're accelerating too late
```

## Chapter 4: Type B — Low Trajectory (Contact Point Problem)

### 4.1 Low trajectory has only two possible causes

```
Shuttle doesn't go high enough = contact point too low OR contact point too far back

No third possibility.

Too low → not enough upward angle → flat trajectory, net-clipping
Too far back → power direction is backward → shuttle goes up but then drops short
```

### 4.2 Which one are you?

```
Hit 5 clears. Have your partner tell you:

☐ The shuttle often clips the net or flies flat
   → Contact point is too low
   → Fix: raise contact point to diagonally above the forehead, not directly above

☐ The shuttle goes high but drops short (good arc but not enough distance)
   → Contact point is too far back
   → Fix: hit farther forward — the racket makes contact in front of the body

☐ The shuttle is sometimes high, sometimes low → unstable
   → Contact point position is not fixed
   → Fix: fix your contact point with shadow practice, then have your partner feed
     consistent tosses
```

### 4.3 The correct contact point position

```
Not "45 degrees forward" — just remember one thing:

The racket face should be about one forearm's length diagonally above and in front of the forehead.

Self-check:
1. Stand in sideways ready position
2. Shadow swing and stop at the contact position
3. Look at where the racket face is relative to your head
4. It should be: diagonally above and in front of the forehead
   (not directly above), about one hand's height above your head

If it's off → adjust to the point where you can generate the most power
with the most comfortable arm extension.
```

### 4.4 Type B self-practice

```
Practice 1: Fixed contact point (3 minutes daily)

Steps:
1. Hang a shuttle from a string at your ideal contact height
2. Stand under it and shadow swing to the contact position
3. Lightly tap the shuttle with the sweet spot
4. Goal: hit the sweet spot every time

Standard:
10 consecutive sweet-spot taps → your contact point is fixed
```

```
Practice 2: Toss-and-decide

Steps:
1. Partner stands on the side, tosses the shuttle near your contact point
2. You don't move your feet — only rotate your upper body to hit
3. Only hit if the shuttle arrives at your "sweet spot" position
4. If it doesn't → let it fall
5. Repeat. This trains your judgment of "which shuttles I can hit well"
```

## Chapter 5: Type C — Wrong Direction (Racket Face Control)

### 5.1 Direction control in one sentence

```
The shuttle goes in the direction the racket face is pointing at the moment of contact.
```

Not the direction your body faces. Not the direction you push.
The racket face direction at contact = the shuttle's direction.

**Direction diagnosis is simple:**

```
Shuttle goes right (right-handed) → racket face is open to the right
  → Cause: insufficient wrist internal rotation (pronation)

Shuttle goes left → racket face is closed to the left
  → Cause: insufficient body side-turn, or excessive arm internal rotation

Shuttle goes left and right randomly → racket face angle is different every time
  → Cause: grip pressure is inconsistent — the racket face stability
    changes with each hit
```

### 5.2 Type C self-check and correction

```
☐ Shuttle always goes right?
   → Wrist didn't pronate (internally rotate) enough at contact
   → Fix: imagine "turning the back of your hand to face up" at contact

☐ Shuttle always goes left?
   → Your body wasn't fully sideways to the net
   → Fix: check if you turned sideways at least 45 degrees

☐ Shuttle goes left and right randomly?
   → Grip pressure is inconsistent
   → Fix: light grip before hitting, tight grip only at contact
          Ensure the same grip pressure every time
```

### 5.3 Type C self-practice

```
Practice: Direction control

Steps:
1. Place a target on the opposite court (shuttle tube, towel, etc.)
2. Hit 10 clears aiming for the target
3. After each hit, record: how far off and which direction
4. If 3 consecutive hits go the same wrong direction → fix per 5.2
5. If direction is random → grip problem

Advanced:
Once you can hit one target consistently → change the target position
Left → right → middle. Practice switching directions.
```

## Chapter 6: Complete Self-Practice Plan — No Coach Needed

### 6.1 Each session structure (20 minutes)

```
Warm-up (3 min)
  Shadow swings ×10 (feel the power sequence)
  Shoulder activation (band external rotation 1 set ×15 reps)

Diagnose (2 min)
  Hit 5 clears → determine your type:
  A (slow) → do Chapter 3 exercises
  B (low) → do Chapter 4 exercises
  C (wrong direction) → do Chapter 5 exercises

Focused practice (10 min)
  Pick ONE exercise from the corresponding chapter
  After every 5 hits → stop → self-check → adjust → hit 5 more
  Don't hit 100 in a row — you need thinking time between each set

Cool-down (3 min)
  Write one sentence:
  "Today my problem was ____, I tried ____, the result was ____"
  Review this sentence before your next practice.
```

### 6.2 Progression standards — when you're "done"

```
Stage 1 (Beginner):
  Hit 10 clears that consistently reach the opponent's baseline area
  (direction doesn't matter)
  Arm not sore after 10 clears
  → You're ready to practice direction control

Stage 2 (Intermediate):
  Can control the shuttle's general direction (left/center/right), deviation < 2m
  No arm or shoulder soreness after 10 clears
  → You're ready to practice timing variation (clear → drive)

Stage 3 (Advanced):
  Freely control the height and landing point of the clear
  Switch between clears and drives at will
  → Your clear is good enough — spend your practice time on other shots
```

### 6.3 What NOT to change

```
Don't change:
- "Is my grip technically correct?"
  If the shuttle goes where you want it to, your grip is fine.
- "Is my backswing big enough?"
  If your power sequence is right, your backswing will naturally be correct.
- "Is my follow-through complete enough?"
  If you finish without pain and without falling over, it's good enough.

Only change:
- Is the power sequence correct? (foot → hip → waist → shoulder → hand)
- Is the contact point correct? (height + forward position)
- Is the racket face control OK? (direction stable)

Everything else is in service of these three.
If these three are correct, your clear is fine.
```

# Shot Pattern Training — Tactical Matchplay Guide

> **Who this is for**: Players whose technique is good enough, but whose shot patterns are predictable, easily read, and who don't know how to structure their offense in a match.
> **Core philosophy**: Shot patterns are not "what shots you can hit" — they are "what shots you can make your opponent hit."

---

## Chapter 1: Shot Patterns Are Not Technique — They Are Methods to Make the Opponent Uncomfortable

### 1.1 Technical Thinking vs Tactical Thinking

| Technical Thinking | Tactical Thinking |
|:-------------------|:------------------|
| "I can hit clears and drops" | "I'll use clears to force him back, then when his weight is on his back foot I'll drop to the net" |
| "His backhand is weak, I'll hit to his backhand" | "I'll hit two consecutive shots to his backhand, then once his stance shifts to the backhand side, I'll suddenly hit to the forehand" |
| "I need to smash to score" | "I'll first make his lifts deteriorate in quality, then smash" |

**Technique solves "how to hit"; tactics solve "where to hit, when to hit, and how to make the opponent run."**

### 1.2 The Three Layers of Shot Design

```
Layer 1: Where do I hit this shot?
  See where the opponent is standing now → hit away from them

Layer 2: After I hit this shot, where will the opponent hit next?
  Based on the quality of your shot and the opponent's habits → predict their return direction

Layer 3: After several consecutive shots, how will the opponent move?
  Use shot patterns to guide the opponent's movement → make them run the way you expect → hit the opposite direction of their run

These three layers build on each other.
Most people never get past Layer 1.
```

## Chapter 2: Deep Court Push/Catch (远网抓推) — Controlling the Game from the Rear Court

### 2.1 What Is "Deep Court Push/Catch"?

```
"Deep court" doesn't mean the shuttle is far from the net — it means —
you're at the back of the court, and the opponent is also at the back of the court.

At this stage, both players are on the baseline. Whoever hits a poor-quality shot first becomes passive.
The core of deep court push/catch is: in this phase, use push drives to control the opponent's movement.
```

**This is NOT about rallying high clears — it's about actively using push drives to change the tempo:**

```
High clear: high arc, slow speed, gives the opponent time to prepare
Push drive: flat arc, fast speed, compresses the opponent's reaction time

Deep court push/catch strategy:
Hit flat clears or push drives from the rear court,
denying the opponent sufficient time to prepare their attack,
while observing their movement habits.
```

### 2.2 When to Push, When to Pull

| Situation | What to Hit | Why |
|:----------|:------------|:----|
| Opponent is deep, weight shifted back | Push to the net (make them run forward) | Running forward when their weight is back is the most uncomfortable |
| Opponent is deep, weight shifted forward | Push to the rear court (make them run back) | Running back when weight is forward is the slowest |
| Opponent is deep, weight centered | Push to the same spot again (make them turn repeatedly) | Consecutive same-direction shots test footwork the most |
| Opponent just hit a shot and is recovering | Push the opposite direction of their recovery | Changing direction while in motion is the hardest |

### 2.3 Deep Court Push/Catch Training

```
Training 1: Push Pressure Practice
Rule: Both players stay at the rear court; only flat clears and push drives are allowed (no high clears)
Purpose: Force yourself to control the opponent with speed and arc
Requirement: 10 consecutive shots without going out or being short counts as passing

Training 2: Push-Drop Combination
Rule: 1 push to the rear court → 1 drop to the net
Purpose: Keep the opponent unable to tell whether you'll push or drop (preparation motion must be identical)
Standard: If the opponent guesses the direction wrong, that's a success for you

Training 3: Deep Court → Net Transition
Rule: Push from the rear court → rush forward to block the net
Purpose: Convert deep-court control into near-net attack
Standard: After pushing, reach the net before the opponent can return the shuttle
```

## Chapter 3: Bait Patterns (诱饵球路) — Let the Opponent Walk Into Your Trap

### 3.1 What Is a "Bait Shot"?

```
Bait shot: deliberately give the opponent a shot that "looks easy to play,"
so they hit the return you expect,
and you're already waiting on that return line to counterattack.

The essence of a bait shot: you control the opponent's decision.
```

**The difference between a bait shot and a normal shot:**

```
Normal shot: you hit it and hope the opponent returns poorly
Bait shot: you hit it and know exactly where the opponent will return it — you're already waiting there
```

### 3.2 Three Common Bait Patterns

**Bait 1: Net Shot Lures a Lift**

```
You play a net shot → opponent lifts → you smash

This is the most basic bait pattern.
You're not playing a net shot to score — you're playing it so the opponent lifts.
Once the opponent lifts, you have a chance to smash.

Key: the net shot quality doesn't need to be great (it doesn't have to roll tightly over the tape),
but after the net shot you must immediately prepare to move back.

If the opponent doesn't lift (plays a net shot or cross-court net shot) → your bait failed.
This means the opponent is better at the net than you — don't keep using this bait.
```

**Bait 2: Half-Court Shot Lures a Smash**

```
You deliberately hit a poor-quality mid-court shot →
Opponent smashes →
You pre-position slightly to one side to defend →
Block the smash and hit to their open space

This bait is for offensive players who "smash every chance they get."
You give them a shot that "looks smashable" and let them smash.
You set up your defensive position in advance, block their smash, and counterattack.

Key: your defensive skill must be able to handle that smash.
If you can't receive it → don't use this bait.
```

**Bait 3: Repeated Pattern Lures a Direction Change**

```
You hit the same direction 3 times in a row (e.g., 3 consecutive shots to the forehand baseline) →
Opponent thinks you'll hit forehand again, shifts their stance to the forehand side →
On the 4th shot, you suddenly hit to the backhand

This bait is for opponents who "are starting to read your patterns."
The first 3 shots genuinely go to that direction (not feints) —
let the opponent build the impression that "he likes hitting forehand,"
then change on the 4th shot.

Key: the first 3 shots must be decent quality. If they're not deep enough,
the opponent doesn't need to adapt to your pattern — because you weren't threatening them anyway.
```

### 3.3 Bait Shot Training

```
Training: 3+1 Training
Rule:
1. First 3 shots hit the same direction (fixed)
2. The 4th shot MUST change direction

Purpose: Practice "first build the opponent's expectation, then break it"
Standard: First 3 shots' landing deviation < 1 meter; the 4th shot's direction change catches the opponent off-guard

Note: During training, hit the first 3 shots seriously too.
Don't slack on them just because "we're going to change anyway."
If the first 3 are poor quality → the opponent won't form a habit → the 4th shot's change is meaningless.
```

## Chapter 4: Push & Lift Combinations (推挑结合) — Controlling the Opponent's Vertical Movement

### 4.1 Tactical Difference Between Push and Lift

```
Push: fast speed, flat arc, gives the opponent a hitting angle but compresses their reaction time
Lift: high arc, slow speed, gives yourself time to recover

Tactically:
Push → pressures the opponent's reaction speed
Lift → pressures the opponent's running distance
```

### 4.2 Four Push & Lift Combination Modes

**Mode 1: Push then Lift (Change of Tempo)**

```
Push 2-3 times in a row (fast tempo) → suddenly lift one high arc (slow tempo)

The opponent just adapted to the fast tempo,
and you suddenly slow it down. They have two reactions:
1. Also slow down → you can suddenly accelerate again
2. Stay fast → they rush their shot and make errors

When to use: when the opponent is too comfortable with your tempo.
```

**Mode 2: Lift then Push (Acceleration)**

```
Lift one high arc (opponent retreats to the rear court) →
As soon as they return it, immediately push a flat arc (compress their reaction time)

The opponent just ran back from the rear court, hasn't stabilized yet,
and your flat push drive is already at their position.

When to use: when the opponent is slow to recover their position.
```

**Mode 3: Push Straight + Lift Cross (Force Running)**

```
Forehand push straight to the opponent's forehand baseline →
They return straight forehand →
You lift cross-court to the opponent's backhand baseline

The opponent has to run from forehand baseline to backhand baseline
(the longest distance on the court).
Two consecutive cycles of this combo → heavy stamina drain on the opponent.

When to use: when the opponent has poor stamina or slow lateral movement.
```

**Mode 4: Push at the Body + Lift to the Gap**

```
Push the shuttle toward the opponent's right shoulder (body shot — hard for them to generate power) →
They passively block the net or lift →
Based on their return direction, lift to the open side

When to use: when you want to force the opponent into an error.
```

### 4.3 Push & Lift Training

```
Training 1: Tempo Change
Rule: 2 pushes → 2 lifts → 2 pushes → 2 lifts (alternate)
Purpose: Practice switching between different tempos
Standard: Actively control the tempo when switching; don't be dragged along by the opponent

Training 2: Fixed Push-Lift Routes
Rule: Push straight → lift cross → push straight → lift cross
Purpose: Practice using push and lift to control the opponent's running direction
Standard: Landing deviation of each push and lift < 1 meter

Training 3: Free Push-Lift
Rule: Freely choose push or lift based on the opponent's position
Purpose: Apply in match conditions
Standard: At least 5 active tempo changes within 10 shots
```

## Chapter 5: Defensive Counter-Attack (防守反击) — Not "Waiting for the Opponent to Make Mistakes," But "Actively Creating Counter-Attack Opportunities"

### 5.1 Defense Is Not Passive

```
The most common mistake among amateur players: defense means "just get the shuttle back."
Then wait for the opponent to make an error.

Correct defense: defense is the process of creating conditions for a counter-attack.
```

**The difference between defense and defensive counter-attack:**

```
Defense: get the shuttle back → wait for the opponent's next shot → get it back again
Defensive counter-attack: use defensive patterns to force the opponent to hit a certain direction →
anticipate in advance → counter-attack at the moment the opponent has the least power
```

### 5.2 Three Defensive Counter-Attack Modes

**Mode 1: Block-Net Counter**

```
Opponent smashes → you block to the net →
Opponent rushes forward to lift or net shot →
You've already moved forward from your defensive position → counter-attack at the net

Key: the quality of your block determines the counter-attack opportunity.
Block tight to the net → opponent can only lift → you can wait at the net for their lift
Block poorly → opponent can net shot or push → your counter-attack opportunity is reduced
```

**Training:**
```
Partner smashes continuously → you block → immediately step forward after blocking →
Partner rushes forward to lift/net shot → you choose to kill or net shot based on the return
Standard: Complete the defense-to-counter transition on 5 out of 10 shots
```

**Mode 2: Cross-Drive Counter**

```
Opponent smashes straight → you drive-drive cross-court →
Opponent runs from forehand to backhand to retrieve the shuttle (passive) →
Your next shot goes the opposite direction of their run

Key: the cross drive must be fast and flat enough — don't give the opponent time to adjust.
```

**Mode 3: Deep Clear Counter**

```
Opponent attacks continuously →
You keep clearing deep (lift to the baseline) →
By the 3rd shot, the opponent's attack quality drops (fatigue or concentration fading) →
On this shot, you counter-attack

When to use: when the opponent's stamina is declining.
Key: the first three clears must reach deep. If the quality is insufficient, the opponent finishes it in one smash.
```

### 5.3 Defensive Counter-Attack Training

```
Training 1: 3 Defends + 1 Counter
Rule: First 3 shots are all defensive (block/lift/drive), the 4th shot MUST counter-attack
Purpose: Practice finding counter-attack opportunities within defense
Standard: The 4th shot's counter-attack transitions you from passive to active (or scores)

Training 2: Defensive Landing Control
Rule: The opponent attacks continuously; you defend to 3 designated positions
      Position 1: Straight net
      Position 2: Cross-court mid-court
      Position 3: Straight baseline
Purpose: Practice controlling the landing point of your defensive patterns,
         instead of "just get it back any way you can"
Standard: 5 consecutive defensive shots land at the designated positions
```

## Chapter 6: Center of Gravity Manipulation (重心调动) — Make the Opponent's Feet Fight Their Body

### 6.1 Position and Center of Gravity Are Two Different Things

```
Position: where the opponent is standing (physical location)
Center of gravity: which direction the opponent's body weight is shifted (movement state)

Hitting to positional space → opponent needs to run there
Hitting the opposite of opponent's weight shift → opponent needs to change direction

Hitting the opposite of their weight shift is far more effective than hitting positional space,
because changing direction is 10 times harder than running in a straight line.
```

### 6.2 How to Read the Opponent's Center of Gravity Direction

```
Right after the opponent hits a shot —
Look at their feet:
  Right foot forward → weight shifted forward (hit to the rear court)
  Left foot forward → weight shifted backward (hit to the net)
  Feet parallel → weight centered (can hit any direction)

Look at their movement trend:
  Running forward → weight shifted forward → hit to the rear court
  Running backward → weight shifted backward → hit to the net
  Moving left → weight shifted left → hit to the right
  Moving right → weight shifted right → hit to the left

Look at their posture after hitting:
  Body leaning forward after shot → weight forward → hit to the rear court
  Body leaning backward after shot → weight backward → hit to the net
  Body leaning left after shot → weight left → hit to the right
  Body leaning right after shot → weight right → hit to the left
```

### 6.3 Center of Gravity Manipulation Shot Design

```
Principle: Make the opponent's feet unable to keep up with their center of gravity.

Specific methods:

1. Front-back manipulation (most common and most effective)
   Hit a rear court shot → opponent moves back → weight shifts back →
   Immediately hit to the net → opponent's weight is back but needs to run forward →
   They need extra force to change their center of gravity direction

   ⚠️ Key: the rear court shot must be deep and well-placed.
   If it's short → opponent doesn't need to fully retreat →
   their weight isn't shifted back → your net shot won't trouble them.

2. Left-right manipulation
   Hit 2 consecutive shots to the same direction →
   Opponent's weight has already shifted to that side →
   3rd shot hits the other direction →
   Opponent needs to start in the opposite direction

   ⚠️ Key: the first 2 shots MUST be the same direction — don't switch.
   1st shot forehand, 2nd shot backhand → opponent's weight never shifts → the 3rd shot has no effect.

3. Front-back + left-right compound manipulation (advanced)
   Rear court forehand → net backhand → rear court backhand → net forehand
   Opponent needs to: retreat → go forward → diagonal run → go forward → diagonal run

   After this 4-shot sequence, the opponent's stamina drain is equivalent to running half the court.
```

### 6.4 Center of Gravity Manipulation Training

```
Training 1: Two-Point Manipulation (front-back)
Rule: You can only hit to two positions — rear court and net — alternating
Standard: Opponent needs to run at least 3 steps every shot

Training 2: Three-Point Manipulation (front-back + left-right)
Rule: You hit rear court → net → backhand rear court, in a loop
Standard: Opponent needs to change direction every shot

Training 3: Free Manipulation
Rule: Based on the opponent's movement trend, hit the opposite of their weight shift
Standard: Opponent makes a "wrong first step" (initially moves the wrong direction)
         at least once every 2 shots
```

## Chapter 7: Match Reading Training (阅读比赛训练) — Knowing Where the Shuttle Is Going Before It Comes

### 7.1 Three Levels of Match Reading

```
Level 1: React after the shuttle is hit
  Opponent hits → you see the shuttle's direction → you start moving
  Result: when the shuttle is fast, you'll never be fast enough

Level 2: Anticipate before the opponent hits
  Observe the opponent's ready stance, backswing direction, racket face angle →
  Judge the shot direction at the moment the opponent makes contact →
  You're already moving in that direction
  Result: you can retrieve most shots

Level 3: Know where the opponent will hit before they even swing
  Combine the score, the opponent's habits, and your own shot design →
  Before the opponent hits, you're already 80% sure where it's going →
  You move into position in advance
  Result: you arrive before the shuttle
```

**Which level are you at?**

```
Self-check: Play 10 points with the same opponent —

If you often say "he hits too fast, I simply can't react in time":
→ You're at Level 1 — you need to go from "watching the shuttle" to "watching the person"

If you often say "I knew where he was going to hit, but my feet couldn't keep up":
→ You're at Level 2 — you read it right, but your footwork isn't there yet

If you often say "I thought he'd hit this side, but he hit the other":
→ You're transitioning from Level 2 to Level 3 — you need to collect more information about the opponent's habits
```

### 7.2 Specific Methods for Training Match Reading

**Method 1: Watch Only the Opponent, Not the Shuttle**

```
Training: You and a partner rally, but your eyes watch ONLY the opponent's body (not the shuttle)
Purpose: Force you to read the shot direction from the opponent's ready stance
Method: You only need to judge the direction (forehand/backhand/center); you don't have to receive the shuttle

Progression: After your direction-judgment accuracy exceeds 80%,
            start adding actual hits (watch opponent → judge direction → move → hit)
```

**Method 2: Fixed-Scenario Prediction**

```
Training: Set up specific scenarios; you must state your prediction aloud before the opponent hits

Scenario 1: Opponent is in a backhand-side passive position → predict where they'll hit
Scenario 2: You played a net shot → predict where the opponent will return it
Scenario 3: You played a deep high clear → predict where the opponent will return it

First say it out loud ("I think he'll hit straight") → then move → check if your prediction was correct
```

**Method 3: Post-Match Prediction Review**

```
After finishing a game, recall:

Which 3 points did you correctly predict?
  What cue from their movement told you?

Which 3 points did you predict wrong?
  What misled you? (Their feint? Your habitual thinking?)
```

### 7.3 Match Reading Progression Path

```
Stage 1 (1-2 weeks): Watch the shuttle, not the person
  You're still chasing the shuttle
  Training focus: start learning to watch the opponent's hitting motion

Stage 2 (2-4 weeks): Watch the person to judge direction
  You start reading the approximate direction from the opponent's ready stance
  Training focus: "where the racket face points = where the shuttle goes"

Stage 3 (1-2 months): Watch the person to judge landing point
  You start reading the shuttle's depth from the opponent's power
  Training focus: "large backswing = rear court, small backswing = net"

Stage 4 (3+ months): Pre-anticipation
  You start predicting shot patterns based on the score and the opponent's habits
  Training focus: build a database of "what they usually hit in this score situation"
```

## Chapter 8: Shot Pattern Combinations Library — Ready-to-Use Match Patterns

The following shot pattern combinations are organized by purpose. Each combination includes: **shot sequence → where you are → what you're waiting for → key point**.

### 8.1 Rear Court Attack (8 patterns)

#### Pattern 1: Deep net shot → catch the lift → smash straight

```
Shot 1: Play a deep net shot (landing near the service line)
Shot 2: Opponent lifts
        You stay in the back court waiting for their lift → side-on smash straight

Where you are: After the net shot, stay in midcourt — do not go to net
What you're waiting for: Opponent's lift
Key point: The purpose of a deep net shot is not to score, it's to force a lift. If it's too tight to the net, the opponent can net it back and you become the one under pressure.
```

#### Pattern 2: Net shot → retreat → smash

```
Shot 1: Play a tight net shot (close to the net)
Shot 2: Opponent lifts → you immediately retreat from net → side-on smash straight or cross

Where you are: After the net shot, retreat immediately
What you're waiting for: Opponent's lift
Key point: After the net shot, shift your weight forward and use the forward momentum to drive a fast retreat.
```

#### Pattern 3: Drive clear to backhand → drop to forehand net

```
Shot 1: Drive clear pressuring opponent's backhand baseline
Shot 2: Opponent returns → you drop to forehand net (their weight is on the backhand side)

Where you are: Back court — maintain attacking position
What you're waiting for: Opponent's weight to shift toward the backhand side
Key point: Shot 1 must be deep enough. If it's not deep, the opponent won't need to retreat fully.
```

#### Pattern 14: Smash cross → net push straight

```
Shot 1: Smash cross (to opponent's backhand side)
Shot 2: Opponent blocks cross to net → you go to net and push straight to forehand baseline

Where you are: Rush to net immediately after smashing
What you're waiting for: Opponent's cross-court block
Key point: After a cross-court smash, the opponent is most likely to block cross. You push straight — the opposite direction of their run.
```

#### Pattern 15: Drop straight → net cross-court

```
Shot 1: Drop straight to net
Shot 2: Opponent plays straight net shot → you go to net and play cross-court net shot

Where you are: Rush to net immediately after dropping
What you're waiting for: Opponent's net return
Key point: After the drop, shift your weight forward and rush in, creating the illusion that you're going straight.
```

#### Pattern 16: Consecutive drive clears to same spot → sudden smash

```
Shots 1-2: Consecutive drive clears pressuring opponent's forehand baseline (same spot)
Shot 3: Opponent's stance shifts toward forehand → sudden smash straight or to backhand side

Where you are: Back court — maintain attacking position
What you're waiting for: Opponent's stance to shift to one side
Key point: The first 2 drive clears must be fast enough that the opponent has no time to adjust their stance.
```

#### Pattern 17: High clear to baseline → drive clear acceleration

```
Shot 1: High clear (slow rhythm)
Shot 2: Opponent returns a high clear
Shot 3: Suddenly accelerate with a drive clear (same direction, flatter trajectory, faster speed)

Where you are: Back court — first 2 shots set the rhythm, shot 3 changes pace
What you're waiting for: Opponent to settle into a slow rhythm, then suddenly accelerate
Key point: The first 2 shots must have exactly the same rhythm, letting the opponent get comfortable with the slow pace.
```

#### Pattern 18: Low defensive drive cross → recover center → net

```
Shot 1: You're at low position in back court, drive cross to opponent's backhand baseline
Shot 2: Opponent runs over to transition → you recover center and go to net

Where you are: Defensive → recover center → net
What you're waiting for: Opponent's transition shot to be average quality
Key point: When you're in a defensive position, don't force an attack. First defuse the situation, then look for an opportunity.
```

### 8.2 Net Control (8 patterns)

#### Pattern 4: Backhand net shot → pre-rotate to catch the lift

```
Shot 1: Backhand net shot (tight to net)
Shot 2: Opponent lifts → you pre-rotate your body and wait → forehand smash or drop

Where you are: After the net shot, don't retreat — turn side-on and wait
What you're waiting for: Opponent's lift
Key point: After the net shot, don't face the net square-on — turn side-on early. If you're square-on and they lift to back court, you can't turn fast enough. If you're side-on, you smash immediately.
```

#### Pattern 5: Spinning net shot → retreat → push drive

```
Shot 1: Spinning net shot (makes the shuttle spin — opponent can't easily net it back)
Shot 2: Opponent can only lift → you take one step back → push drive to opponent's backhand baseline

Where you are: After the spin, take one step back to midcourt
What you're waiting for: Opponent's lift
Key point: Enough spin → opponent won't dare net it → you're 100% sure they'll lift.
```

#### Pattern 6: Push to waist → net kill follow-up

```
Shot 1: Push the shuttle to the opponent's waist area (flat, not high)
Shot 2: Opponent blocks or lifts → you move forward to intercept at net

Where you are: After pushing, step forward immediately
What you're waiting for: Opponent's block — intercept and kill at net
Key point: Push to waist means a flat push, not a high one. Push too high → opponent has time to prepare.
```

#### Pattern 19: Repeated net shots → force a lift → smash

```
Shot 1: Net shot → opponent nets back → you net again (same spot)
Shot 2: Opponent is forced to lift → you retreat and smash

Where you are: Net rally → retreat and smash
What you're waiting for: Opponent to break first and lift
Key point: This is a battle of net shot consistency and patience, not technique.
```

#### Pattern 20: Consecutive push drives

```
Shot 1: Push to opponent's forehand baseline
Shot 2: Opponent blocks net → you go to net and push again to backhand baseline
Shot 3: Opponent's quality drops after continuous running → you smash

Where you are: Midcourt push → net → back court smash
What you're waiting for: Opponent's return quality to decline after continuous running
Key point: Every push must reach the baseline. If it falls short, it's ineffective.
```

#### Pattern 21: Lift → receive smash block net → net follow-up

```
Shot 1: Lift to opponent's baseline
Shot 2: Opponent smashes → you block to net
Shot 3: Opponent comes to net and lifts → you follow in and push or kill

Where you are: Defensive → block then immediately go to net
What you're waiting for: Opponent to finish their smash and come to net to lift
Key point: The block must be tight to the net, giving the opponent no chance to net it back.
```

#### Pattern 22: Cross-court net shot → opponent nets straight → push to back court

```
Shot 1: Cross-court net shot (from forehand net to backhand net)
Shot 2: Opponent plays straight net shot → you push straight to back court

Where you are: After the cross-court net shot, immediately adjust position ready to push
What you're waiting for: Opponent's straight net return
Key point: After the cross-court net shot, immediately prepare to move in the straight direction.
```

#### Pattern 23: Fake net shot → real push

```
Shot 1: You show the preparation for a net shot → opponent's weight shifts forward →
        At the moment of contact, you change to a push to the back court

Where you are: At the net
What you're waiting for: The moment the opponent's weight shifts forward
Key point: The preparation motion for the net shot and the push must be completely identical. Only change the racket face at the very last moment.
```

### 8.3 Movement Manipulation (8 patterns)

#### Pattern 7: Repeat same corner → suddenly change to opposite

```
Shot 1: Forehand baseline → Shot 2: Same forehand baseline →
Shot 3: Suddenly change to backhand baseline

Where you are: Back court — maintain attacking position
What you're waiting for: Opponent's stance to shift toward the forehand side
Key point: Shots 1 and 2 must land at the exact same spot — deviation less than 1 meter.
```

#### Pattern 8: Net → deep → net (front-back big movement)

```
Shot 1: Play a net shot → Shot 2: Lift to back court → Shot 3: Play another net shot

Where you are: Running constantly between front and back
What you're waiting for: Opponent's stamina to drop or return quality to decline
Key point: This pattern only works if your stamina is better than the opponent's.
```

#### Pattern 9: Smash straight → net cross-court

```
Shot 1: Smash straight → Shot 2: Opponent blocks straight to net
        You go to net → play a cross-court net shot

Where you are: Rush to net immediately after smashing
What you're waiting for: Opponent's straight block
Key point: You MUST rush to net immediately after smashing. If you stay in place after smashing, you can't recover after the cross-court net shot.
```

#### Pattern 24: Three-point movement

```
Shot 1: Forehand back court → Shot 2: Backhand net → Shot 3: Forehand net

Where you are: You don't need to run — your shots make the opponent run
What you're waiting for: Opponent to be unable to reach in time on the 3rd shot
Key point: 3 consecutive shots in different directions with no break in between, making the opponent go all-out on every shot.
```

#### Pattern 25: Consecutive repeat landing (3+ times)

```
Shots 1-3: Backhand baseline → Shot 4: Still backhand baseline

Where you are: Back court — repeatedly hitting the same target
What you're waiting for: Opponent's patience and concentration to break down
Key point: After 3 consecutive shots, the opponent starts thinking, "Here again?" By the 4th, their mentality crumbles.
```

#### Pattern 26: Hard smash mixed with soft smash (power alternating)

```
Shot 1: Hard smash → Shot 2: Opponent defends at full power →
        You soft smash (60% power) → Shot 3: Opponent's rhythm is broken

Where you are: Back court — alternating between hard and soft smashes
What you're waiting for: Opponent to adapt to your smash rhythm, then suddenly change speed
Key point: The preparation motion for hard and soft smashes must be completely identical.
```

#### Pattern 27: Short-medium-long combination

```
Shot 1: Net shot (short) → Shot 2: Mid-court shot (medium) → Shot 3: Back court (long)

Where you are: Controlling three depths
What you're waiting for: Opponent to handle the mid-court position poorly
Key point: The short → medium → long rhythm makes the opponent always one step behind.
```

#### Pattern 28: Consecutive cross → sudden straight

```
Shot 1: Cross-court high clear → Shot 2: Cross-court drop →
Shot 3: Opponent's stance shifts toward cross side → you suddenly smash straight

Where you are: Back court to mid-court control position
What you're waiting for: Opponent to habitually lean toward the cross side
Key point: The first 2 shots should be forehand-side cross shots (maximum movement distance).
```

### 8.4 Defensive Counter-Attack (7 patterns)

#### Pattern 10: Receive smash block net → follow up → net kill

```
Shot 1: Opponent smashes → you block straight to net
Shot 2: Opponent comes up and lifts → you follow in → kill or push

Where you are: Defensive → move forward immediately after blocking
What you're waiting for: Opponent's lift
Key point: The block must be tight to net — don't give opponent a chance to net it back.
```

#### Pattern 11: Receive smash drive cross → recover center → change line

```
Shot 1: Opponent smashes straight → you drive cross to opponent's backhand baseline
Shot 2: Opponent runs over and return quality drops → you hit the opposite direction of their run

Where you are: Defensive → recover center
What you're waiting for: Opponent's return quality to drop after running
```

#### Pattern 29: Consecutive deep clears → sudden net shot

```
Shots 1-2: Opponent attacks continuously, you keep clearing deep
Shot 3: Opponent expects another deep clear → you suddenly play a net shot

Where you are: Defensive position → suddenly change rhythm
What you're waiting for: Opponent to get used to your defensive pattern
Key point: The first 2 deep clears must reach the baseline. Otherwise the opponent doesn't need to retreat fully.
```

#### Pattern 30: Two consecutive net shots

```
Shot 1: Opponent smashes → you net shot
Shot 2: Opponent net shots → you net shot again (tighter)
Shot 3: Opponent is forced to lift

Where you are: Defensive → net
What you're waiting for: Your net shot quality to surpass the opponent's
Key point: The 2nd net shot must be better quality than the 1st.
```

#### Pattern 31: Anticipate opponent's cross smash → block straight

```
Shot 1: Lift to opponent's backhand baseline
Shot 2: Opponent is likely to smash cross → you pre-position slightly toward cross side → block straight

Where you are: Pre-move slightly toward the cross side
What you're waiting for: Opponent's cross smash (based on observing their habits from previous points)
Key point: This is not a guess — it's a judgment based on observation. First watch which direction they smash from their backhand position.
```

#### Pattern 32: Receive serve push drive → consecutive drives → change line

```
Shot 1: Receive serve, push drive → Shots 2-3: Consecutive drives →
Shot 4: Opponent's drive quality drops → you suddenly change line

Where you are: Midcourt drive position
What you're waiting for: Opponent's drive to slow down or get higher
Key point: The quality of your serve receive push drive determines whether you can dominate the drive rally.
```

#### Pattern 33: Defensive lift → receive smash drive → net

```
Shot 1: Defensive lift → Shot 2: Opponent smashes →
        You receive smash and drive cross → Shot 3: Go to net and control

Where you are: Defensive → sudden counter-attack → net
What you're waiting for: Opponent to expect you to keep defending
Key point: The more defensive you are, the more you should think, "How do I counter-attack?" Don't just think, "Get it back."
```

### 8.5 Doubles (8 patterns)

#### Pattern 12: Short serve → third shot drive to back court

```
Shot 1: Serve short to opponent's backhand side
Shot 2: Opponent nets or lifts → you or your partner drives to back court

Where you are: After serving, stay at net ready to intercept
What you're waiting for: Opponent nets → intercept; opponent lifts → drop back and attack
Key point: Don't retreat after serving — prepare to intercept at net.
```

#### Pattern 13: Drive middle → switch to sideline

```
Shots 1-3: Drive repeatedly down the middle (between both opponents)
Shot 4: Opponents start closing toward center → push to sideline

Where you are: Midcourt drive position
What you're waiting for: Both opponents to shift toward center — hit the sideline gap
Key point: The quality of the first few drives determines how well the switch works.
```

#### Pattern 34: Flick serve → block net → partner intercepts

```
Shot 1: Flick serve (back court)
Shot 2: Opponent smashes → you block to net
Shot 3: Your partner at net intercepts and kills

Where you are: After serving, drop back to defend → block net
What you're waiting for: Opponent's smash
Key point: After a flick serve, you and your partner have clear roles — you defend, partner intercepts at net.
```

#### Pattern 35: Receive serve push to waist → net intercept

```
Shot 1: Receive serve, push to waist (push between both opponents)
Shot 2: Opponent handles it poorly → you and your partner both move forward to intercept at net

Where you are: Immediately step forward after receiving the serve
What you're waiting for: Opponent to handle the waist-area shot poorly
Key point: Push to waist means flat and fast, pushed between both opponents but slightly toward one player.
```

#### Pattern 36: Drive to drop shot

```
Shots 1-2: Drive rally with opponent
Shot 3: Mid-drive motion, suddenly reduce power and turn it into a drop shot

Where you are: Midcourt drive position
What you're waiting for: Opponent to enter "drive mode"
Key point: The preparation motion for the drive and the drop must be completely identical.
```

#### Pattern 37: Smash combined with soft smash (doubles version)

```
Shot 1: Hard smash → Shot 2: Opponent blocks net →
        You soft smash (50% power) → Partner at net kills

Where you are: Back court attacking position
What you're waiting for: Opponent's defensive stance to be too low
Key point: After the hard smash, the opponent gets used to one speed → the soft smash suddenly slows down → their rhythm is completely broken.
```

#### Pattern 38: Rotational attack

```
Shot 1: You smash from back court, partner at net
Shot 2: Opponent blocks tight to net → you go to net to take it, partner drops back to back court
Shot 3: You net shot or push, partner at back court ready to attack

Where you are: You rotate from back court to net, partner from net to back court
What you're waiting for: The opponent is most vulnerable when hitting down the middle during rotation
Key point: As you go to net, your partner drops back simultaneously. Shout "switch" so your partner knows.
```

#### Pattern 39: Receive smash lift to back court → rotational defense

```
Shots 1-2: Opponent smashes continuously, you keep lifting to back court
Shot 3: You and your partner rotate defensive positions (straight ↔ cross)

Where you are: Defensive position rotation
What you're waiting for: Opponent's stamina to drop after continuous attacking
Key point: During continuous defense, rotate positions with your partner to disrupt the opponent's attacking habits.
```

### 9.2 One Theme Per Game

```
Don't just play every game "by feel."
Before each game starts, give yourself a shot pattern theme.

Game 1 theme: "I'll mainly use center of gravity manipulation — pull him front and back"
Game 2 theme: "I noticed his backhand is weak — 70% of my shots go to his backhand"
Game 3 theme: "His stamina is dropping — I'll use defensive counter-attack and wait for his errors"

Stick with one theme all the way through.
Don't change themes just because you lost 2 points.
Unless you have clear evidence the theme isn't working,
otherwise, stick with it for at least one full game.
```

### 9.3 Shot Pattern Growth Path

```
L1: Can only play one shot pattern
  Characteristic: Only clears, or only smashes
  Problem: Opponent adapts quickly
  Breakthrough: Choose one new pattern, practice until match-ready

L2: Can play multiple patterns but can't combine them
  Characteristic: Knows clear, drop, smash — but transitions are clumsy
  Problem: No connection between patterns
  Breakthrough: Pick one combo (e.g., clear + drop), use it deliberately in matches

L3: Can combine 2-3 patterns
  Characteristic: Can execute fixed combos effectively in matches
  Problem: Combos are predictable
  Breakthrough: Increase variation (at least 3 options for the same scenario)

L4: Can design patterns
  Characteristic: Can flexibly design patterns based on opponent and match situation
  Problem: Occasional misjudgment
  Breakthrough: Build an opponent database (record every opponent you play)

L5: Automated pattern design
  Characteristic: Patterns change naturally based on opponent without conscious thought
```

**Where are you now?**

```
Self-check — think about your last match:

Did your shot patterns make the opponent uncomfortable?
  □ Yes, they often guessed wrong → L3+
  □ No, they could basically predict my patterns → L2 or below

Do you know your opponent's weaknesses?
  □ Yes, I specifically target their weaknesses → L3+
  □ No, I just hit the shuttle back → L2 or below

Can you say "what I learned from this game" after a match?
  □ Yes → L4
  □ No → Start keeping records
```
